01

What this project actually is

SCI-001

The tool answers one question, every day, for Lake Balaton: is the water surface unusually warm — or cold — right now, compared with what's normal for this exact time of year?

That question drives almost every decision below, because answering it honestly means being careful about three things: what "normal" means, what counts as a trustworthy measurement, and how to say "warmer than normal" without hiding uncertainty or overselling the result.

02

Where the numbers come from

DATA-001DATA-002DATA-003DATA-004

Two NASA satellites carrying the MODIS instrument give a long, four-times-daily record of the lake's actual surface temperature. Each pass is kept as its own separate stream, shown in the order they happen through a date (Hungarian clock time; winter is ~1 h earlier):

Aqua — pre-dawn
~03:00 (Hungary)
Terra — mid-morning
~11:00 (Hungary)
Aqua — early afternoon
~14:00 (Hungary)
Terra — evening
~21:30 (Hungary)

The pre-dawn Aqua pass for a given date is taken in the small hours of that date, so it is the first of the four, not the last. A lake heats through the day and cools overnight — mixing "how warm at 2 pm" with "how warm at 3 am" into one number would hide the real signal, not reveal it (more in §08).

ERA5-Land reanalysis (air temperature, wind, sunlight) is now shown as weather context in the single-day view (§10) — never a replacement for the satellite reading. Two more sources are approved for later: Landsat (sharper, 30 m images, for zooming into a specific hot day), and any field measurements, an optional cross-check only, since a thermometer in the water measures something subtly different from what a satellite sees from orbit.

03

Defining "normal"

TIME-001

One "normal" is worked out once, from 1 Jan 2003 through 31 Dec 2022 — and it never changes. Every day from 2023 onward, including today, is measured against that exact same, frozen reference. A fixed ruler, not one redrawn every year.

2003 is the first full calendar year where both Terra and Aqua were already flying, so it's the earliest point where all four streams have a complete, comparable record.

Two other designs were seriously considered — a baseline that keeps growing (2026 measured against 2003–2025), and one that rolls forward, always the trailing 20 years (2026 against 2006–2025). Here's why the fixed baseline wins on the question this project is actually asking:

Fixed 2003–2022Growing baselineRolling 20‑yr window
Same reference every yearYesNo — grows yearlyNo — slides yearly
Recent warm years can't quietly raise "normal"YesNoNo
A real warming trend stays visibleYesPartly hiddenPartly hidden
Matches WMO climate-normal practiceYesNoPartly

The failure mode both alternatives share has a name in climate science: shifting baseline — a reference that keeps updating with recent, already-changed conditions gradually hides the very change you're trying to measure. 2024 turned out to be the warmest year on record for every satellite pass; folding it into the next year's "normal" would use that heat to quietly raise the bar for what counts as warm. A growing baseline also breaks a second thing: 2023 would be judged against 20 years of history, 2026 against 23 — different rulers, so you could no longer honestly say which year was more anomalous.

Why this is standard, not just a preference

Meteorological agencies define "climate normals" the same way — the WMO uses fixed 30-year windows, redefined only once per decade, never year to year. If this baseline is ever updated, the right way is a deliberate, periodic redefinition — not a yearly creep.

04

Deciding which readings to trust

QA-002AUDIT-011AUDIT-012

This is the part people ask about most — why does the daily map have holes in it? Short answer: most holes are clouds that NASA removed before we ever saw the data, and the rest are pixels we chose not to trust. Here is that answer in slow motion, plain first, with the formal term beside each step.

4.1 · The picture you're actually working with

Each satellite pass is one snapshot, taken in about five minutes as the satellite flies over Hungary — not a blended or "best of the day" picture. Whatever the sky is doing in those five minutes is what you get.

ExampleIf a band of cloud drifts across the western basin at 10:02 when Terra passes, the western basin is missing from that day's Terra-morning image — even if it was perfectly clear an hour earlier.

Formal: the MOD11A1 (Terra) / MYD11A1 (Aqua) daily 1 km land-surface-temperature product — one instantaneous overpass per satellite per day, not a temporal composite.

4.2 · Clouds are removed before we ever see the data

Before this project touches anything, NASA has already run its own cloud detector on the image. Any pixel it judges cloudy is left blank — NASA doesn't even try to compute a temperature there. So most holes in the daily map are simply "there was a cloud here," decided upstream, not by us.

ExampleScattered fair-weather clouds give a Swiss-cheese pattern of small blank dots. A weather front gives a clean diagonal edge — clear on one side, blank on the other.

Formal: NASA's MOD35 cloud mask, applied upstream. In the pixel's quality byte this shows up as mandatory_qa = 2 ("LST not produced due to cloud"). A sibling code, mandatory_qa = 3, means "not produced for another reason" — thin cloud, aerosol, a missing atmospheric-correction input — also blank, also not our call.

4.3 · The report card on every surviving pixel

Pixels that do arrive with a temperature carry a one-byte "report card" — four separate grades, each a number from 0 (best) to 3 (worst):

What the grade really asksFormal field
Did a usable value get produced — and roughly how good?mandatory_qa
Is it in focus — a solid retrieval or a shaky one?data_quality
Did we guess the surface material right — open water vs. reed vs. mud?emissivity_error
How many degrees might we be off — ±1 K? ±2 K? ±3 K?lst_error

All four grades are packed into a single 8-bit number, which is why the code does bit-shifting — that's just unpacking the four grades back out of the one byte.

What "emissivity error" means, since it's the least obvious one: MODIS never measures temperature directly. It measures how much thermal infrared light the surface is glowing with, then converts that glow into a temperature. That conversion needs one assumption — how efficiently does this surface radiate? — and that efficiency is called emissivity. Water radiates efficiently and very consistently (about 0.99), so MODIS's assumption for open water is almost always right. Reed beds, wet mud and dry shore soil each radiate differently, and MODIS has to guess which one it is from a land-cover map. The emissivity_error grade is MODIS saying "how sure am I that I used the right radiating efficiency here?" For Balaton this is the least worrying grade — most pixels are clean open water — but it's exactly the one that degrades along the shoreline, where a pixel is half water, half reed.

4.4 · Our own sanity checks

On top of MODIS's report card we add three cheap "is the housekeeping intact" checks — these mostly catch fill values and corrupt metadata, not bad weather:

  • Raw temperature value in a physically possible range — rejects the fill value 0 and impossible numbers. raw DN in [7500, 65535]
  • Overpass time present and sane. view time in [0, 240]
  • Viewing angle present and sane. view angle in [0, 130] — this one also carries real meaning: a pixel seen from the far edge of the swath is viewed through a long slanted slice of atmosphere and its footprint is smeared larger, so an extreme angle is genuinely lower quality. The rule just checks it's present and in range rather than penalising oblique views directly.

4.5 · The bar we set — and why not stricter

MODIS grades each pixel; we decide which grades to accept. The rule keeps a pixel only if it has a real temperature (not cloud, not "failed for another reason"), the retrieval is in focus (best data_quality), the material is known to within ~2% (emissivity_error at best or second-best), the reading is accurate to within about 2 K (lst_error at best or second-best), and the three housekeeping checks pass. Everything else we discard — which turns it into a hole on our map even though MODIS gave us a number.

Why not demand a perfect reading

It was tested. With the strictest rule (every grade must be 0), Lake Balaton has 0% usable nighttime coverage — every night, across a full year of test dates. Allowing the "within 2 K" tier recovers ~99%, and 98.7% of those recovered nighttime pixels carry the exact same quality byte — 65 = produced-but-flagged / good data quality / emissivity error ≤ 0.02 / LST error ≤ 2 K.

Formal ("candidate C"): mandatory_qa ≤ 1 AND data_quality == 0 AND emissivity_error ≤ 1 AND lst_error ≤ 1, plus the provider-range checks from §4.4. Applied identically to day and night — daytime already clears the strict bar, so one shared rule costs it nothing and is simpler to audit than two.

Reporting "no data, every night, forever" would silence half the product for no real accuracy gained — the discarded pixels aren't bad measurements, they just miss an overly strict paperwork threshold. And nighttime anomalies matter: February 2024 nights ran +5.1 °C above normal.

4.6 · The kinds of holes

What you see on the mapEveryday cause
Big blank regions with clean edgesThick cloud — upstream, the dominant cause
Blank patches on a clear-looking dayThin haze / aerosol / a missing correction input — NASA couldn't retrieve
The lake cut off along one straight lineThe satellite's swath edge clipped the lake, or there was no pass that day
Scattered single missing pixels on an otherwise full lakeIndividual pixels we rejected as too noisy (~1% at night)
Rare stray gapsMissing quality byte, or a fill value in the temperature / time / angle

4.7 · What the leftover pixels produce — and the honesty flag

We average whatever accepted pixels remain into that pass's lake temperature — even if only a handful survived. Then we label how much of the lake we actually saw:

none — saw 0 pixels low — saw under 15% of the lake ok — saw 15% or more
ExampleAn August Terra-evening pass with only 25 clear pixels out of ~700 → 3.6% of the lake → a temperature is still reported, but flagged low, because 3.6% of the lake isn't really "the lake."

Formal: valid_water_fraction = accepted pixels ÷ ~700 lake pixels; tiers none / low (< 0.15) / ok. Night passes are structurally low here — averaging only ~10% of the lake even on good nights — which is why the low-coverage mark shows up so often on the pre-dawn and evening rows. Every nighttime record also stores what the strict rule would have shown, so nothing is hidden.

4.8 · Two things spotted while looking at the map, investigated, not "fixed"

Reviewing the app turned up two visual patterns worth explaining properly, even though neither changes the accepted-pixel rule above. Full evidence, every date and number: docs/CLOUD_EDGE_AND_FOG_ARTIFACTS_NOTE.md.

Pattern one — a cold ring around cloud holes, in the daytime. Zoom in on the edge of a cloud-shaped gap in the map and the pixels right at the border are often a touch colder than pixels a few cells further in — a faint cold rim around the hole, not a flat temperature up to the mask's edge.

ExampleThink of a MODIS pixel as one small photograph, 1 km on a side. A cloud doesn't line up with that square's edges — right at a cloud's boundary, a pixel can be mostly clear lake but partly cloud. The camera can't split that into two readings; it reports one blended brightness for the whole square. Cloud tops are far colder than the lake surface, so even a small sliver of cloud mixed into the pixel drags its apparent temperature down — and NASA's own cloud test doesn't always catch a contamination that small, so the pixel still gets waved through as "clear." We confirmed this on four separate dates (28 Aug 2026 near Balatonfüzfő / Siófok / Balatonakarattya included) — pixels right next to a hole ran 0.8–3.1 °C colder than pixels further from it, every time there was a real, moderate-sized gap to check.

Formal: sub-pixel cloud contamination that survives MOD35's cloud test, concentrated at cloud edges where optical thickness tapers rather than cutting off sharply. QA-002 runs downstream of that test and cannot re-detect it. Not correctable by tightening the pixel filter — that trade-off (less coverage for less edge risk) was already rejected once, for the same reason: it would shrink the already-thin nighttime record further.

Pattern two — a "salt-and-pepper" scatter before dawn, probably fog. Some pre-dawn passes don't show one clean gap with a cold rim at all — instead, the temperature jumps between colder and warmer almost at random from one pixel to its neighbour, like a checkerboard instead of one solid cloud shape, and only a fifth or so of the lake comes through clearly.

Example — 18 August 2026, Aqua pre-dawnThree separate checks, like a small detective story: (1) the jumpiness itself — neighbouring pixels differed by about 1 °C on average, roughly twice as jumpy as a normal daytime cloud edge (0.45 °C); real cloud usually forms one coherent shape, not scattered noise. (2) we checked the regional weather model for that hour — it said the sky was mostly clear, only 13% cloud. But MODIS still rejected 78% of the lake. A model can't be that wrong about a large storm system — so whatever MODIS saw was smaller than the model's 31 km grid cells can even represent. (3) we checked how close the air was to its dew point (the temperature at which mist starts to condense) — only 3.2 °C away, versus 6.6–11.7 °C on three clear comparison nights. Air that close to saturation, on a calm night after hours of cooling, is exactly when shallow fog forms over a lake — patchy, sitting right on the water, each little patch smaller than MODIS's 1 km squares, and a light breeze that night (4.2 m/s) would have stirred it into drifting patches rather than one smooth sheet. Put together: not a storm the model missed, but fog too small and too local for anything except MODIS's own per-pixel test to notice — and even that test only half-catches it, which is exactly the scattered pattern on screen.

Formal: hypothesised shallow radiation fog, sub-pixel and spatially discontinuous, undetected by ECMWF/ERA5/HOURLY total_cloud_cover (~31 km) but partially flagged by MODIS's thermal-only nighttime cloud test. Evidence: neighbour-pixel roughness ≈2× a daytime cloud-edge case; ERA5 regional cloud fraction 13% vs 78% MODIS rejection; ERA5-Land dew-point depression 3.2 °C vs 6.6–11.7 °C on three clear control nights. This is what motivated the "Air vs dew point" fog-risk reading now in the weather panel (§10) — the one weather signal that still means something at night, since sunshine needs daylight to say anything at all.

05

What counts as "the lake"

SPACE-001

The whole lake, as one polygon — no basin subdivision (Keszthely / Szigliget / Szemes / Siófok), and no inward shrinking of the shoreline.

No basins because no official Hungarian basin boundary exists to use — the academic supervisor couldn't point to one either, and inventing one is a real scientific choice that a six-week internship shouldn't make silently. It's documented as future work instead.

No shoreline buffer because it was actually tested, across four shrink distances (0 m to 927 m): shrinking inward only lowers the already-scarce nighttime coverage further, and Balaton is narrow enough that aggressive shrinking cuts out whole cross-sections of open water. And because this is an anomaly product, the same near-shore mixed pixels sit in both the historical baseline and today's reading — so most of the land-mixing bias cancels rather than skewing the anomaly. If validation later finds a specific artifact, this is revisited — it's the best-evidenced choice today, not a permanent one.

06

Building one day's anomaly

METH-001METH-002

For one satellite pass, one day, three numbers get compared: what was actually measured today, what's typical for this calendar day, and the gap between them.

The typical value comes from every year 2003–2022, using a ±5-day window around the date — for 19 February, every 14–24 February, 2003 through 2022. Checked directly: at ±5 days almost every calendar day has a full 20 years of history and 110–195 individual readings behind it — a solid sample, wider than an exact-day match alone, narrower than a window that would start blending different parts of the season.

Median first, mean also shown. The median — "half of past years were warmer, half cooler, on this kind of day" — is the headline. The plain average is computed too, but as a secondary reference. Why: a mean gets pulled around by occasional bad pixels and by skew, and Balaton's nighttime readings are measurably skewed — the median sits up to 2.6 °C above the mean on about 60% of the year. Leading with the mean would make nighttime anomalies read systematically too warm, from statistics alone, not from anything happening at the lake. A wide gap between the two is itself useful: it flags that a given day's history is unusually skewed.

anomaly = measured temperature − reference (median or mean)
Positive = warmer than normal  ·  Negative = colder than normal. Shown as, e.g., "+6.1 °C warmer than normal" with the exact reference value spelled out underneath — never a bare, directionless number.
07

Percentile, then one label

METH-003METH-004TERM-001

A raw °C gap alone doesn't say how unusual it is — +2 °C can be ordinary in one season and remarkable in another. So every reading also gets ranked against its full historical sample (the same 110–195 readings from §06), using the standard Type 7 percentile method. Minimum-sample guardrails exist in the method (e.g. no percentile below 10 historical readings) purely for an unusual data gap — on the real lake-wide product, with 110–195 readings behind every day, they essentially never trigger.

That percentile becomes exactly one plain-language label:

below normal within normal range warm unusually warm extreme warm observation

Deliberately not called a "heatwave" or "thermal event" unless a properly validated method for sustained multi-day events exists — one warm day is a different, smaller scientific claim than a persistent heatwave, and the wording never overstates what a single reading supports.

The label is a hard cut — so the app shows the percentile too

Validation (§11) found ~7 % of well-observed days sit right on a band boundary — say the 94.8th percentile, one notch below "unusually warm". A legitimate method tweak (a ±3-day window instead of ±5) can nudge such a day across, flipping the word even though the temperature barely moved — always by a single notch, never a jump. The app now prints the percentile beside the label ("95th percentile · unusually warm") so a borderline reading looks borderline. The bands themselves were not changed.

08

Why the four streams never merge

METH-006

Aqua-pre-dawn, Terra-morning, Aqua-afternoon and Terra-evening are always shown side by side — each with its own temperature, anomaly, percentile, and label. There is no single combined "today's lake temperature."

They aren't measuring the same thing. A lake genuinely warms through the day and cools at night — an unremarkable afternoon and a genuinely unusual warm night are two different physical stories, and averaging them would blur both. A carefully designed combined index remains possible future work, but only if it can be built without hiding this distinction.

09

Monthly summaries

METH-005QA-001

A month's summary — mean temperature, mean anomaly, warmest day, biggest single-day jump, count of warm-or-above days — is computed per satellite pass, for the same reason as §08, and only from days that already passed the §04 confidence check at low or better.

A month needs at least 3 qualifying days before it reports anything; otherwise it says so plainly — "insufficient valid observations" — rather than quietly computing a number from one lucky clear day. Nothing is ever filled in or guessed for a missing day; a cloudy month just visibly has fewer points behind its summary.

10

The public app's own decisions

PERF-001ARCH-001

Sections 03–09 are what the app shows. A separate layer of decisions governs how, refined directly against real use:

  • It reads pre-computed results and never recalculates 20 years of history live — the baseline is built once and stored permanently; only new days/months get appended in a manual monthly refresh.
  • A calendar-style date picker, not a raw numeric slider — with the recalculation deliberately delayed until you stop moving, so scrubbing through many days triggers one calculation, not one per day passed over.
  • The whole-month view has its own Month selector, separate from the daily calendar, since every day inside a month gives the same monthly answer.
  • The whole-month map is a bias-corrected monthly composite. The problem: the obvious way to draw it — average each pixel over the days it was clear — is skewed, because clouds over Balaton aren't random. Warm days are the clearer ones, so they contribute far more pixels than cold days do; the result ("clear-sky bias") is a map ~0.5 °C warmer than the true monthly mean, every month, that would visibly disagree with the number beside it. Two options: (a) show that and note it, or (b) correct it — (b) was chosen, a systematic every-month error isn't something to knowingly display. The correction: every reading = that day's lake average + how far the pixel sat from it. The day-to-day level (which carries the bias) is stripped — each day's pixels have that day's own lake average subtracted, leaving only the small, roughly bias-free offsets. Those are composited per pixel, then the day-weighted monthly figure (the readout number, no clear-sky bias) is added back. Result: the same within-lake pattern as the plain composite, but the overall level now matches the headline number to ~0.05 °C — far below the product's own ~1–2 °C accuracy.
  • Once a month's or year's data is fetched, browsing inside it is instant — only the map's satellite image still needs a fresh request per day, since that's genuinely a new picture each time.
  • The map's colour scale is fixed at −5 °C to 32 °C, never rescaled to a single day's own min/max — otherwise a mild day and a truly extreme one would look equally red. The small legend is cropped to the current view's own range, so you can see where it sits within that fixed scale, without ever renormalising the colours themselves.
  • Every internal decision code you see as a chip on this page is deliberately absent from the app itself — useful here, meaningless to someone just checking today's temperature.
  • The classification label always shows its percentile next to it, and the four-pass table shows what share of the lake each pass saw — both added after validation (§11) so a reading near a threshold, or one from thin coverage, looks that way rather than reading as a clean fact.
  • The four passes are shown in the order they happen — Aqua pre-dawn (~03:00 Hungarian time, the first reading of the date, not the last), Terra ~11:00, Aqua ~14:00, Terra ~21:30. Times are the mean measured overpass times over the lake (an hour earlier in winter); the old labels were rounded and off by up to ~1.5 h.
  • A "Weather" panel (single-day view) shows the ERA5-Land reanalysis conditions for the selected pass — air temperature and wind at the overpass hour, plus the day's sunshine and rain. It is context for why a reading was unusual (calm + sun lets the surface skin run hot, or cold before dawn; wind mixes it away); it is never a measurement of the water (DATA-003). Sunshine is shown as "% of a clear day" (see glossary) so it compares across seasons.
  • The panel also shows "Air vs dew point" — how close the air is to saturation, the standard fog-risk indicator. Sunshine only describes daylight, so it says nothing about a night pass; the dew-point gap works at any hour. Added after investigating a noisy pre-dawn pass (§4.8) — see docs/CLOUD_EDGE_AND_FOG_ARTIFACTS_NOTE.md for the evidence.
11

How it was validated

VAL-001

No thermometer-in-the-water data for Lake Balaton was available, so the product was checked seven other ways. All seven passed; VAL-001 was approved in September 2026.

  • It behaves like a real lake. The 2003–2022 "normal" is a smooth annual cycle (~0 °C in winter, ~24 °C in July), daytime warmer than night in summer, and the four passes correlate with each other at r ≥ 0.98.
  • An independent instrument agrees. Our monthly averages were compared against Landsat surface temperature — a different thermal sensor — for all 258 months from 2003 to 2024. They agree to within ~0.5 °C on average and move together at 99 % correlation. A weather model (ERA5-Land) tracks it too. Landsat and ERA5-Land were used here only as measuring sticks — they are not part of the product.
  • The flagged anomalies match the record. Every standout — February 2024, March 2024, summer 2024, 2024 as the warmest year — lines up with the Copernicus European climate bulletins.
  • The answer doesn't hinge on arbitrary choices. A ±3 or ±7-day window instead of ±5, a stricter pixel filter, or a trimmed shoreline each move the anomaly by only a fraction of a degree.
  • The coverage flag works. Every one of the ~200 wild single-day spikes built from a handful of cloud-gap pixels is correctly marked "low coverage".
What it is validated for

Telling you whether a reading is unusual for its time of year, and how much to trust it — a relative claim. It is not calibrated to the exact temperature to a fraction of a degree; that would need in-situ data. Documented limitations: ~7 % of well-observed days sit on a percentile boundary (so the app now shows the percentile next to the label); a handful of borderline-coverage days are flagged for a manual look; and the strict pixel filter is confirmed to be the wrong choice for this lake, as QA-002 decided.

Glossary

Anomaly
How far today's measurement sits from what's typical for the time of year — measured minus typical; positive means warmer than normal.
Median
The middle value of a sorted list — half above, half below. Resistant to a few extreme or bad readings.
Mean
The plain average. Sensitive to extreme values and to a skewed distribution.
Percentile
Where a value ranks among a historical group, from 0 (coldest on record) to 100 (warmest on record).
Skin temperature
What a satellite thermal sensor actually measures — the top, sub-millimetre surface layer. It can differ slightly from a thermometer a few centimetres into the water, which is why the two aren't treated as interchangeable.
QC / quality flags
Extra information shipped with every satellite pixel describing how trustworthy that specific reading is. In MODIS LST it is a single byte packing four 0–3 grades: mandatory_qa, data_quality, emissivity_error, lst_error (see §04.3).
Emissivity
How efficiently a surface radiates thermal infrared light compared with a perfect radiator (1.0). A satellite measures the glow, then needs an assumed emissivity to turn that glow into a temperature. Water's is high (~0.99) and stable, so it's well known; mixed land/water shoreline pixels are where the assumption gets shaky. emissivity_error is MODIS's own estimate of that uncertainty.
LST
Land Surface Temperature — for a lake, the temperature of the water skin. MODIS's daily products are MOD11A1 (Terra) and MYD11A1 (Aqua), 1 km.
Coverage / valid-water fraction
The share of the lake's ~700 pixels that gave an accepted reading on a given pass. Drives the none / low / ok confidence flag.
"% of a clear day" (weather panel)
The day's measured solar energy at the ground (ERA5-Land) ÷ the cloudless maximum for that date and the lake's latitude, ×100. The cloudless maximum is calculated, not measured: Sun geometry (latitude + day of year → energy at the top of the atmosphere over the day) × 0.75 for what a clean, cloudless sky lets through — the standard FAO-56 clear-sky formula. "74%" ⇒ clouds blocked about a quarter of the day's available sun. Comparable across seasons, unlike raw kWh/m². Clamped to 100% in the app; rough by ±a few %, not a measurement.
Shifting baseline
The problem where a "normal" that keeps updating with recent, already-changed conditions gradually hides the very change you're trying to measure.
